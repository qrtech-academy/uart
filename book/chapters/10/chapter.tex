\chapter{Integration and Bring-Up}
\label{c10:ch}

{\large\itshape Both halves on the bench; the pyramid gets its top.\par}

\begin{chapterbox}{In this chapter}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Two serial links}, carrying different traffic over different protocols. SPI, from
    the Nano to the DE0-CV through the level shifter, is the \textbf{control plane}: the ATmega
    driving the peripheral's registers. The peripheral's own UART \code{tx} and \code{rx} are the
    \textbf{data plane}, running out to a 3.3\,V USB-serial adapter.
  \item \textbf{Level shifting}, since the Nano is 5\,V and the DE0-CV 3.3\,V, together with the
    four SPI lines and the wiring table.
  \item \textbf{The bring-up ladder}, climbed one rung at a time, each rung proving something the
    previous one could not.
  \item \textbf{\code{app::EchoNode}}, written and host-tested against the UART stub of \chapref{6}
    first, and only then run on the ATmega over your own VHDL peripheral, with one byte traced end
    to end.
\end{itemize}
\end{chapterbox}

Everything the book built meets on one bench, and the first thing to get straight is that two serial
links run across it. After this chapter you should be able to write \code{app::EchoNode} against the
driver interface and host-test it on the UART stub of \chapref{6} with no hardware, then bring it up
as the final rung of the ladder; wire the two-link bench correctly, level shifter included, and
explain what each SPI line and each UART line carries; climb the bring-up ladder methodically and
state what each rung establishes that the previous one cannot; and explain what an integration test
catches that no host test can, and where the top of the test pyramid sits for this system.

\textbf{Before you start}, you need a \code{uart_top} from \chapref{5} that passes the system
testbench, synthesized and programmed onto the DE0-CV in \chapref{9} through the board wrapper
(\file{uart_board.vhd}) in the Quartus project, and the ATmega firmware of \chapref{9}, flashed, with
its host tests passing. You also need the hardware listed in the preface's \emph{Setting up}: a
DE0-CV, a Nano, a level shifter, a 3.3\,V USB-serial adapter, and, recommended but not required, a
logic analyzer.

Read \secref{c10:sec:bench} for the two links, level shifting and the board wrapper. Then read
\secref{c10:sec:echo_node}, and write and host-test \code{app::EchoNode} (Exercise~\ref{c10:ex:b1})
before you go near the bench; that needs no hardware, only the driver interface and the UART stub of
\chapref{6}. Because the class arrives at the bench already proven, the only open questions there are
wiring and links. Read \secref{c10:sec:exercises} for the wiring tables and the bench procedure, and
build the wiring table yourself from the SPI parameters in Part~3 of the protocol
(\secref{proto:part3}) and the board pinout. Have it checked before powering anything.

The work at the bench is climbing the \textbf{bring-up ladder}, stopping at the first rung that
misbehaves. Each rung adds exactly one layer, so the first rung that breaks names the culprit; the
rungs are Exercise~\ref{c10:ex:c3}. Rung a is a \textbf{data-plane pin loopback}: the board wrapper
wires its \code{rx} pin straight back to its \code{tx} pin, bypassing the peripheral, so a PC
terminal echoes itself and only the adapter, the levels, the wiring and the pin assignment are on
trial (not the terminal's baud rate: the adapter sends and receives at the same setting, so a loopback agrees with itself at any rate). Rung b brings up the \textbf{control plane}, writing \code{BAUD_DIV} over
SPI and reading it back. Rung c is \textbf{peripheral loopback}, with \code{tx} tied to \code{rx} on
the board so that the driver can send a byte and read the same byte back without the outside world.
Rung d is the \textbf{real data plane}, the same send and receive against the terminal. Rung e is
\textbf{EchoNode}, the full application.

The order is forced: the peripheral cannot transmit until \code{BAUD_DIV} is written, and
\code{BAUD_DIV} is only reachable over SPI, so the control plane must be proven before any rung that
uses the peripheral.

The chapter closes with Exercise~\ref{c10:ex:c4}: trace a single byte through every layer, from the
PC terminal through the FPGA's UART receiver, the FIFO, the register bank, the SPI bridge,
\code{AvrSpi}, the driver and \code{EchoNode}, and back out again, naming each module and the
chapter that built it.
